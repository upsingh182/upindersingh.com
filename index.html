01 · Build with AI

I didn’t wait for the tools.
I built them.

Weeks → days

Product decisions took weeks. I built the AI system that made it days.

The problem

Product decisions on my small-business internet portfolio were slow. Turning an idea into a decision took weeks. The data lived in a dozen systems, and even “how many active customers do we have” came out three different ways. Context was buried in 170–220 messages a day across 20–40 team chats. Teams spent meetings reconciling numbers instead of acting on them. Off-the-shelf AI didn’t fix it: every chat started from zero, and a tool that invents a number is worse than no tool at all.

What I did

I designed and built my own AI operating system for product work, from scratch, on Claude and Google’s tools. A lead agent routes each question to one of about 10 specialist agents covering areas like retention, growth, pricing and operations. One of them exists only to attack every plan before it reaches leadership. A decision ledger keeps a running record of what was decided, why, and on what evidence, so nothing is forgotten between conversations. Underneath it, I joined three separate systems into one dataset on Google Cloud Platform (GCP).

The decision

I built in two rules most AI setups skip. The first: autonomy is proven, not assumed. A new agent only drafts, every number needs a source, and I approve. As its track record builds, it moves up approval tiers and handles routine calls on its own; if its scores slip, it moves back down. The second: every agent has to earn its place. I started with 20, saw them overlapping and blurring who owned what, and cut back to about 10. Each is now scored on what it adds, and the weak ones get merged or retired.

The result

Ideation to product decision went from weeks to days. Three conflicting customer counts became one trusted number. Twenty-five data disputes went into a single log, and each one was tracked to an answer instead of being re-argued. Two hundred messages a day became one ranked brief. A weekly leadership report that took half a day now builds itself from live data.

I let AI handle the legwork. The decisions, and the accountability, stay with me.

02 · Intuition, backed by data

Intuition guides.
Data anchors.

Any system is only as good as the numbers going into it. Reporting what a number says is easy. I’ve spent fifteen years asking why it says that, and what it costs the business if nobody asks.

$21M saved in year one

$1B a year, planned on a spreadsheet that was wrong 4 times in 10

The problem

Our Fortune 500 client spends about $1B a year on infrastructure in one region alone. Their leadership set us an audacious goal: cut that spend by 10% within two years and improve customer experience at the same time. But the static, outdated spreadsheet-based solution they used to decide where to upgrade got only 59% of the calls right. Money went into upgrades nobody needed, while places that did need one were missed and customers there lived with slow service.

What I did

I was the lead product manager for a Bay Area AI startup’s flagship machine learning product: a forecaster that studies years of usage history to predict where demand will outgrow what’s in place, so money goes there first. The existing solution ran on rules that looked back only 18 months, so I trained our model on 36, long enough to tell a one-off spike from real growth. From there I kept tuning the model’s parameters so it got sharper with every round. Before any change counted, I ran it against new builds still waiting for funding, to see which ones the model would back and which it would hold.

The decision

As product manager, I decided which signals the model should weigh most, drawing on years in the industry and my read of that market. Then, instead of a polished demo, I proposed a head-to-head on their own data: our model against their in-house tools, on the same sites over the same four months. It tested the method and built trust in the same move. If the model was as good as I believed, their planners would see it in their own numbers.

The result

In year one, the first version (MVP) alone saved $21M, 2.1% of the yearly budget. The client’s spreadsheet got about 6 of every 10 upgrade calls right; our model got 8 (59% → 81%). Upgrades nobody needed dropped from 213 to 76. Upgrades that were needed but missed dropped from 121 to 49. It remains one of the startup’s headline products. Full results are in the white paper I co-authored with a Big Four consulting firm.

A $0 fix to a record cancellation spike

The “demand problem” that wasn’t

The problem

I owned the internet product portfolio for small businesses at one of Canada’s largest tech companies, about $10M a year in revenue. In 2026, cancellations ran 40% higher than the year before, while the customer base grew only 24%. Then came a record month of cancellations. Small businesses were leaving at the fastest pace we’d seen, and each one took recurring revenue with it. Leadership needed an answer before the holiday sales peak. The room’s first guesses were price and product quality.

What I did

The data had been sitting there all along; nobody had pulled it apart. I did it myself, querying the raw data on Google Cloud Platform (GCP). I broke down every cancellation by how the customer was sold and when their contract ended. The spike wasn’t spread across the business. Most of it came from subscriptions sold as add-ons to business phone plans, now reaching the end of two-year contracts. More than half of those businesses had never used the internet service once. My investigation ended at a badly placed sales discount. A business got a discount on its phone plan if it also had internet with us, with no minimum spend. So sellers added our product to unlock the discount, and customers ended up paying for a service they never needed. Two years later, they left.

The decision

Fixing the discount meant fewer new sales in the short term, which is a hard sell when teams are measured on volume. I made the case to directors as value over volume: every customer signed through that loophole was a cancellation two years out. My fix cost nothing: set a minimum spend to qualify for the discount. To protect the customers we keep, I built a churn prediction model, which flags new customers who look likely to leave while there’s still time to win them back.

The result

I won director buy-in across 5 teams. The minimum-spend rule went live two weeks before Black Friday, so the busiest sales season of the year brings in customers who actually want the service. This is a deliberate long-term play: the payoff shows up as those contracts renew instead of cancel. My churn model tracks exactly that, so the gain doesn’t quietly slip away.

03 · Revenue from products customers love

Keeping the right customers is half of it. The other half is finding the ones nobody is building for yet.

+76% revenue in year two

The customers who’d pay more to do less

The problem

I owned the internet product for small businesses. Most small businesses want to be in control, so self-install was the default, and it worked for them. But in my experience, about 1 in 5 want the opposite. They have the budget, but not the time, the know-how or the appetite to climb a ladder. Our offer was built for the do-it-yourself majority, so these businesses had nothing designed for them. That meant revenue we weren’t capturing, from customers ready to pay for the right service.

What I did

I went to them. I visited and interviewed 20 small businesses across British Columbia and asked two things: what would it take for you to install this yourself, and what would white-glove service mean to you? The first answer was a firm no: no time, no climbing ladders, no risk of getting it wrong. The second answer gave me a spec. Then I ran support calls through an LLM (a large language model) to find where hands-off customers struggled and when. Together, that showed exactly what this group needed, and that they’d pay for it.

The decision

I made it a paid premium tier, not a free add-on. This group valued the service, and pricing it that way turned their need into a new revenue stream. I mapped the customer journey end to end, from first order to first bill, and rallied tech, marketing, sales and support behind it. Ordering got simpler. A concierge booked the install for the customer. Technicians arrived with the ladders, brackets and mounting frames they needed to finish in one visit. Seven days later, we circled back to make sure we’d given them the service they’d paid a premium for.

The result

Revenue from the tier grew 76% in its second year. Premium subscribers grew 5× since launch, and their customer satisfaction scores rose 15 points.

Who I am as a product manager

  • The customer defines easy. Full control for some, done-for-you for others. I build with the whole picture in view, and weigh every choice against revenue.
  • Why, then so what? “Why?” tells me what’s really going on. “So what?” tells me whether it matters, and what it costs if nobody acts.
  • Data is my north star. Every decision starts with the numbers, and every launch is measured against them.
  • Judgment finds the route. Data shows where to go. Judgment turns an option that looks good on paper into one that works in the real world, and sometimes into an answer no spreadsheet would suggest.
  • I own it, so I use every tool. The outcome is mine, so I reach for whatever gets it done, from an AI agent to a plain conversation with the customer. Often the simplest one wins.
